Foundation Concept

1. Meaning and Definition

Modern meaning

A computer is an electronic device that accepts data (input), processes it according to a set of instructions (program), stores results and produces output. It can perform arithmetic, logical and control operations at extremely high speed.

In simpler terms: a computer is a programmable machine that can automatically carry out sequences of calculations or operations.

Original (historical) meaning

Long before machines existed, a “computer” was a person whose job was to perform mathematical calculations, often in teams. This human role was common in astronomy, navigation and engineering until the mid-20th century.

Language & History

2. Where the Word Comes From

The word traces back to Latin:

  • Latin computāre
  • com- (“together”) + putāre (“to think, reckon, prune, settle an account”)
  • Literal meaning: to count up, calculate, or reckon together.

The Latin verb entered French as computer (same meaning) and then English borrowed it. The English noun computer originally meant “one who computes.”

Historical Terminology

3. First Recorded Use of the Word “Computer” (for a person)

The earliest known written use in English appears in 1613, in a book by Richard Braithwait titled The Yong Mans Gleanings. He wrote:

“I haue read the truest computer of Times and the best Arithmetician that euer breathed and he reduceth thy dayes into a short number.”

Here, “computer” refers to a person skilled in calculation - specifically, a man who computes dates and numbers (likely a reference to God as the ultimate reckoner of time).

Evolution of Meaning

4. When the Word First Referred to a Machine

The shift from person to machine happened gradually.

1897
Late 19th century: The Oxford English Dictionary records the first use of computer for a calculating device in 1897. The reference appears in the magazine Engineering, describing a mechanical analog device: “The computer of the machine…”. This referred to a part of an apparatus that performed computations, not a general-purpose electronic machine.
1930s–1940s
As electromechanical and electronic calculating machines developed (e.g., the Harvard Mark I, ENIAC), the term increasingly meant the machine itself. Human computers worked alongside these early machines.
After WWII
With the rise of stored-program electronic digital computers (like EDVAC), the modern meaning became dominant. The word no longer needed “electronic” tacked on; computer now meant a machine by default, while “human computer” faded as a job title.

Key Historical Idea

6. Who “Coined” the Modern Sense?

No single person coined the machine meaning in a deliberate way - it evolved naturally from the human job title. However, influential figures shaped its adoption:

  • Alan Turing provided the conceptual blueprint of a programmable computer.
  • John von Neumann outlined the practical architecture and the term computer was already used for machines like ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, 1946), cementing the modern usage.
Early Computing Tool

7. The Abacus (c. 2400 BCE – 500 BCE)

Definition and Description

The abacus is one of the oldest known calculating devices in human history. It is a manual computing tool consisting of a wooden frame with rods or wires strung with movable beads. The earliest abacuses are believed to have originated in Mesopotamia around 2400 BCE, with the Chinese version known as the “suanpan” emerging later.

A traditional Chinese abacus has a rectangular wooden frame with a central beam (girder) dividing it into two parts: the upper section called “Heaven” and the lower section called “Earth”. Each rod typically has two beads in the upper part (each representing five) and five beads in the lower part (each representing one).

How It Works

The abacus works on a place-value system. Each vertical rod represents a different decimal place (units, tens, hundreds, etc.). To represent a number, beads are slid toward the central beam-beads moved toward the beam are counted, while those moved away are not.

  • Beads in the upper deck (Heaven) each count as 5
  • Beads in the lower deck (Earth) each count as 1

By moving beads up and down according to specific rules, users can perform addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. The abacus requires the coordination of mind, eyes and hands, making it an effective tool for developing mental arithmetic skills.

Mechanical Calculator

8. Pascal's Calculator (Pascaline) – 1642

Definition and Description

The Pascaline was the first mechanical calculating machine, invented in 1642 by the French mathematician and physicist Blaise Pascal (1623–1662). Remarkably, Pascal invented this device when he was only 19 years old. He created it to help his father, who worked as a tax collector, with tedious arithmetic calculations.

The Pascaline was a brass or metal box containing a series of cogged (toothed) wheels, with each wheel representing a decimal digit. The machine typically had six or eight dials visible on the top surface, corresponding to units, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten-thousands and hundred-thousands.

How It Works

To use the Pascaline, the operator would use a stylus (a small pointed tool) to turn the numbered dials. Each dial displayed digits 0 through 9 around its circumference.

  • Addition: Turning the dials clockwise added numbers
  • Subtraction: Turning the dials counter-clockwise subtracted numbers

The machine’s ingenious feature was its automatic carry mechanism. When a dial turned from 9 to 0 (completing a full rotation), a mechanism called the “sautoir” (a gravity-driven lever) would automatically advance the next higher digit wheel by one position. This “carry” operation was entirely mechanical and happened without any manual intervention.

Babbage’s Mechanical Computing

9. Babbage's Difference Engine (1822–1833)

Definition and Description

The Difference Engine was designed by Charles Babbage (1791–1871), a British computing pioneer often called the “father of computing”. Babbage first conceived the idea in 1812 when he realized that mathematical tables (used in navigation, astronomy and engineering) were full of human errors. He wanted to create a machine that could calculate and print these tables without error.

Work on the Difference Engine began in 1823 and a portion was assembled in 1832 by Joseph Clement, a skilled toolmaker. However, the project was abandoned in 1842 due to lack of funds. The machine was a decimal digital device-the value of a number was represented by the positions of toothed wheels marked with decimal numbers.

How It Works

The Difference Engine was based on the method of finite differences-a mathematical technique that calculates complex polynomial functions through repeated addition, without requiring multiplication or division.

  • One column stored the initial value of the table
  • Additional columns (up to six) stored the successive differences

When the operator turned a crank handle:

  1. The machine would add the differences incrementally
  2. Each addition produced the next value in the mathematical table
  3. The results could be printed automatically

When a toothed wheel turned from nine to zero, it caused the next wheel to advance by one position-similar to the carry mechanism in Pascal's calculator.

General-Purpose Computer Concept

10. Babbage's Analytical Engine (1834–1871)

Definition and Description

The Analytical Engine, conceived by Babbage in 1834, was his most visionary creation and is generally considered the first computer. Unlike the Difference Engine (which could only perform specific calculations), the Analytical Engine was designed as a general-purpose, fully program-controlled, automatic mechanical digital computer.

Though Babbage worked on it until his death in 1871, only a small portion was ever built. If completed, the Analytical Engine would have been over 4 meters tall and 6 meters long, probably powered by steam.

ComponentFunctionModern Equivalent
MillPerformed arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division)CPU (Central Processing Unit)
StoreHeld numbers and intermediate resultsMemory/RAM
ReaderRead instructions and data from punched cardsInput device
PrinterOutput resultsOutput device

The Store was designed to hold 1,000 numbers, each of 50 decimal digits.

How It Works

  1. Input via Punched Cards: Programs and data were entered using punched cards-an idea Babbage borrowed from the Jacquard loom. Different cards controlled different operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, etc.).
  2. The Mill (Processor): This was the “brain” of the machine. It could perform all four arithmetic operations, plus comparisons. The Mill and Store were connected by axes called “Ingress” and “Egress” for transferring data.
  3. The Store (Memory): Numbers were stored on vertical columns with figure wheels.
  4. Conditional Control: The machine could perform conditional branching and loops-meaning it could make decisions and repeat operations, just like modern computers.

Remarkably, the Analytical Engine had a processor, a memory and a way to input information and output results-the same fundamental architecture used in every computer today.

Input & Recording Technology

11. Punched Cards – Definition and Working Principle

Definition

A punched card (also called a punch card or Hollerith card) is a recording medium made of thin cardboard that stores information through the presence or absence of holes in predefined positions. First used in 1725 for controlling textile looms in France, punched cards became the primary medium for data storage and input in early computing from the 1920s through the 1950s.

How Punched Cards Work

The punched card system is based on a simple binary principle: hole = 1, no hole = 0.

The Jacquard Loom (1801)

Joseph-Marie Jacquard invented a loom in 1801 that used punched cards to control the weaving of intricate patterns. The mechanism worked as follows:

  1. A continuous chain of punched cards was fed into the Jacquard head
  2. A set of pins was pressed against each card
  3. Where there was a hole, the pin passed through and activated a hook, raising the corresponding warp thread
  4. Where there was no hole, the pin was blocked and the thread remained lowered
  5. The sequence of raised and lowered threads created the woven pattern automatically.

Hollerith's Tabulating Machine (1890)

Herman Hollerith adapted the punched card concept for data processing, using it to tabulate the 1890 U.S. Census. His system worked as follows:

  1. Punching: Operators used a pantograph or keyboard punch to create holes in cards, with each hole representing a specific piece of data (age, gender, marital status, etc.)
  2. Reading: Cards were fed into a tabulating machine
  3. Spring-loaded metal pins were pressed against the card
  4. Where there was a hole, the pin passed through and made electrical contact with a pool of mercury below
  5. This completed an electrical circuit
  6. Each completed circuit advanced a mechanical counter by one
  7. Results: The counters displayed the tabulated data, allowing rapid processing of millions of records

Hollerith's company later became part of IBM and punched cards remained the standard for computer input well into the 1970s.

Computer Organization

12. Block Diagram of a Computer and Its Components

A block diagram of a computer is a simplified, high-level visual representation of the major functional units of a computer system and the data flow between them. It abstracts away complex hardware details to show how information enters, gets processed, is stored and is output.

Modern computers are based on the Von Neumann Architecture (or its later modification, the Harvard Architecture), which fundamentally consists of a processing unit, a memory unit and input/output mechanisms.

Key to the arrows

  • Solid arrows typically represent the flow of data.
  • Dashed arrows (implied from CU to ALU/Memory) represent the flow of control signals (the CU telling other parts when and how to act).
Definitions + Functionality

13. Detailed Breakdown of Components

Here is the proper definition and key functionality of every major component shown in the diagram.

1. Input Unit

Definition: The input unit is a collective term for all hardware devices used to transfer data and instructions from the outside world (the user or external environment) into the computer system.

Key Functionality

  • Conversion: It converts user-friendly data (like keystrokes, mouse movements, or audio) into binary code (machine language, i.e., 0s and 1s) that the computer can understand.
  • Transmission: It sends this converted data to the Primary Memory (RAM) for temporary storage before the CPU processes it.
  • Common Examples: Keyboard, Mouse, Scanner, Joystick, Microphone, Touchscreen.

2. Central Processing Unit (CPU)

Definition: The CPU is the “brain” and “heart” of the computer. It is an integrated circuit (microprocessor) responsible for interpreting instructions and processing all data contained in the software.

Key Functionality: It performs all arithmetic, logic and control operations according to a program's instructions. It operates on the Fetch-Decode-Execute Cycle:

FetchGets the next instruction from memory.
DecodeFigures out what the instruction means.
ExecutePerforms the instruction.

A. Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU)

Definition: The ALU is the digital circuit within the CPU that handles all mathematical and logical operations.

Key Functionality

  • Arithmetic Operations: Performs basic mathematics such as addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.
  • Logical Operations: Performs comparisons and decision-making, such as AND, OR, NOT and comparing whether a value is greater than or less than another. The results of these logical operations dictate the program's branching (e.g., deciding to run an “If” or “Else” block).

B. Control Unit (CU)

Definition: The CU is a component within the CPU that directs the operation of the processor. It does not execute instructions itself; rather, it tells the other parts how and when to execute them.

Key Functionality

  • Instruction Decoding: Translates fetched instructions into a series of electronic signals.
  • Traffic Management: Generates precise timing and control signals that synchronize the ALU, memory and input/output devices. Control signals include :
    • 1. Memory Read (MR or RD)

      Definition: A control signal sent from the Control Unit (CU) to the Primary Memory (RAM).

      Function: Directs the memory to place the data stored at the specified memory address onto the Data Bus, allowing the CPU to read the data.

      Used During: The Fetch stage, when the CPU retrieves an instruction from memory and during Load operations, when data is transferred from memory to the CPU.

    • 2. Memory Write (MW or WR)

      Definition: A control signal sent from the Control Unit (CU) to the Primary Memory (RAM).

      Function: Directs the memory to store the data currently available on the Data Bus at the specified memory address.

      Used During: Store operations, when the CPU saves data or a calculation result into memory.

    • 3. Input Read (IOR - Input/Output Read)

      Definition: A control signal sent from the Control Unit (CU) to an input device or input port.

      Function: Directs the input device to place the available input data onto the Data Bus so that the CPU can read it.

      Used During: Input operations, when the CPU receives data from an external input device.

    • 4. Output Write (IOW - Input/Output Write)

      Definition: A control signal sent from the Control Unit (CU) to an output device or output port.

      Function: Directs the output device to accept the data currently available on the Data Bus and use it as required.

      Used During: Output operations, when the CPU sends data to an external output device.

  • Instruction Sequencing: Maintains a counter to determine the address of the next instruction to be fetched, ensuring that instructions are executed in the correct sequence.

    • 3. Memory Unit (Primary / Main Memory)

    Definition: The primary memory is the internal storage area directly accessible by the CPU. It holds the data and instructions that the computer is actively using.

    Key Functionality: It provides high-speed, temporary storage with extremely fast read/write access to feed data to the CPU.

    Two main types

    • RAM (Random Access Memory): Volatile memory (data is lost when power is off). It holds the current operating system, running applications and active data.
    • ROM (Read-Only Memory): Non-volatile memory (retains data when powered off). It contains the BIOS/UEFI (firmware) needed to boot up the computer and initialize hardware during startup. Data here is permanently written during manufacturing and cannot be easily modified.

    4. Secondary Storage (Auxiliary Memory)

    Definition: Secondary storage refers to external, non-volatile storage devices that permanently store large amounts of data and programs, even when the computer is turned off.

    Key Functionality

    • It serves as the computer’s long-term “filing cabinet.”
    • Because it is much larger in capacity than RAM, it stores the operating system, software applications, documents, photos and videos.
    • Data from secondary storage must be loaded into Primary Memory (RAM) before the CPU can process it, as the CPU cannot directly process data from a hard drive.
    • Common Examples: Hard Disk Drives (HDD), Solid State Drives (SSD), USB flash drives and CDs/DVDs.

    5. Output Unit

    Definition: The output unit is a collective term for all hardware devices used to display, present, or transmit the processed results generated by the computer to the outside world (the user).

    Key Functionality

    • Conversion: It takes the binary results (machine code) from the Primary Memory and converts them into human-readable formats (text, visuals, audio, or physical movement).
    • Peripheral Control: It drives the physical hardware to produce the final output.
    • Common Examples: Monitor (VDU), Printer, Speakers, Projector and Plotters.
Communication Between Units

14. The Flow of Data and Communication (The System Bus)

For the components to work together, they communicate via a System Bus-a set of parallel electrical wires connecting the CPU, Memory and I/O devices. It consists of:

Data BusCarries the actual data (numbers/text) between components. It is bidirectional.
Address BusCarries the memory addresses from the CPU to the memory (telling the memory where to read or write data). It is unidirectional (from CPU to Memory).
Control BusCarries the control signals (timing and commands) from the Control Unit (CU) to synchronize and manage the read/write operations across all units.
Core Data Processing Model

15. The Complete Data Processing Cycle of a Computer [Input Process Output Storage (IPOS) Cycle]

The Data Processing Cycle (often called the IPOS Cycle) is the systematic sequence of operations that converts raw data (unprocessed facts) into meaningful information (useful, organized output). In modern computing, this cycle is a continuous, repetitive loop-as the output of one process often becomes the input for the next.

Note: This is a continuous cycle. The arrow from Storage loops back to Input because saved information is often pulled out of storage to become raw data for a new processing run (e.g., running monthly reports on saved sales data).

Stage-by-Stage Breakdown

Stage 1: INPUT (Data Collection & Preparation)

Definition: The INPUT stage is the act of capturing, gathering and preparing raw, unprocessed data from the real world and translating it into a machine-readable format suitable for the computer system.

Key Functionalities

  • Data Collection/Gathering: Acquiring raw facts from various sources (sales receipts, sensor readings, survey forms, or user keystrokes).
  • Data Encoding/Conversion: Translating human data into binary (0s and 1s) so the CPU can understand it. For example, pressing “A” on a keyboard sends the binary code 01000001 to the computer.
  • Data Verification & Validation: Performing initial checks to ensure data is accurate and complete before it enters the core system. (e.g., checking if a date field is correctly formatted).
  • Input via Hardware: Physically entering the data through Input Units (Keyboard, Scanner, Barcode Reader, Microphone).

Stage 2: PROCESSING (The Core Transformation)

Definition: The PROCESSING stage is where the Central Processing Unit (ALU + Control Unit) performs operations on the raw input data to transform, manipulate and convert it into a more meaningful and organized form.

Key Functionalities (executed inside the CPU and RAM)

  • Arithmetic Calculations: Performing math operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) on numerical data (e.g., calculating a sales total).
  • Logical Comparisons & Sorting: Comparing values to make decisions (e.g., “If stock < 10, reorder”) and arranging data alphabetically or numerically.
  • Merging & Summarizing: Combining multiple sets of data (e.g., merging a customer list with an order list) and condensing large amounts of data into averages, sums, or reports.
  • Data Interpretation: Taking coded data and translating it into a usable state (e.g., interpreting the database query instructions).

This stage heavily relies on the Fetch-Decode-Execute cycle performed by the CPU discussed in the previous block diagram.

Stage 3: OUTPUT (Presentation of Information)

Definition: The OUTPUT stage is the dissemination of processed results from the computer system back to the user or to another external system in a human-readable or actionable format.

Key Functionalities

  • Translation: Converting the processed binary results from the primary memory (RAM) back into human-understandable formats (text, images, graphs, sound).
  • Presentation: Delivering the final “Information” to the end-user via Output Units (Monitor displays a chart, Printer produces a hard copy, Speaker plays an alert).
  • Action Triggering: Sometimes output doesn't go to a human but directly triggers a physical action (e.g., a computer-controlled CNC machine starts cutting metal, or a motor turns on).

Stage 4: STORAGE (Retention for Future Use)

Definition: The STORAGE stage is the process of saving the Input, Processing commands and Output results onto a non-volatile, permanent medium so they can be securely retained and retrieved at a later time.

Key Functionalities

  • Temporary vs. Permanent: Temporary (Primary Storage/RAM) holds data only while the machine is powered on and actively processing it; Permanent (Secondary Storage - HDD, SSD, Cloud) retains data indefinitely after power-off.
  • Archiving: Compressing and storing old data for legal or historical compliance.
  • Indexing & Organization: Structuring saved data within a File System or Database so it can be located quickly.
  • Retrieval (The Loop): When the user wants to run a new process on existing data, the Retrieval operation pulls it out of Storage and feeds it back to the INPUT stage, completing the cycle.

Fast Revision

17. Why Is It Called a “Cycle”?

It is called a cycle because it is continuous and iterative.

  • An organization processes daily sales data (Input) → calculates weekly reports (Processing) → prints/sends emails (Output) → saves the report to a database (Storage).
  • The next morning, that saved report is retrieved (Input) and compared against the new day's targets (Processing) to generate new insights (Output).
Unit summary: The complete Data Processing Cycle ensures that data flows logically through four distinct phases, transforming it from raw, chaotic facts into structured, actionable intelligence, while permanently preserving it for historical analysis and future operations.
